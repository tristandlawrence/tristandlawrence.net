Outside of work, and inside it

Games

Games and interactive media aren't only fun. They're educational, and they're productive in a work setting.

Why games

I enjoy experiencing stories and challenges I don't get to experience in real life — that's the obvious part. The less obvious part is that I think games are genuinely useful. Used well, they help people collaborate: in a workplace, and in society more broadly.

A game gives people permission to try things. It compresses consequences into a few hours, lets people fail safely, and makes the shape of a problem visible in a way a slide deck never does. That's why I use them at work as well as on weekends — for threat modelling, for incident training, and for teaching people how decisions actually get made under pressure.

Games at work

Threat modelling workshop

An open-source, game-based threat modelling workshop I developed with colleagues at MYOB and have iterated on since. Participants roleplay threat actors against a real system, then prioritise, risk-assess and decide what to do about what they find. It's designed for rapid collaboration between engineering and security teams, and it's free to use — the materials, including the threat actor cards, are on GitHub.

Incident walkthrough training

Developed with the University of Melbourne — guided walkthroughs that put people inside a cyber security incident and make them make the calls.

Current project: an incident card game

I'm working with Isaac at the University of Melbourne on a card game that better simulates the two things that make real cyber incidents hard: nobody has the full picture, and coordinating across people who each hold a fragment of it is the actual challenge.

Megagaming

Megagames are one-off, large-scale live-action games — usually 50 to 200 players split across teams, playing out a scenario in real time over several hours. I facilitate, run logistics and lead games for Melbourne Megagames, and I've been on the board since 2025.

Scaling a volunteer organisation that runs events this size is the same problem I solve professionally: clear roles, decisions made fast under uncertainty, and a design that survives contact with a room full of unpredictable people. The work that makes it repeatable — logistics, facilitator development, and structure that doesn't depend on the same three people every time — is the part I care most about.

Tabletop

I dungeon master and play Dungeons & Dragons and other tabletop role-playing games. Running a table is the purest version of the skill I use everywhere else: hold the structure loosely enough that people can surprise you, tightly enough that the story still goes somewhere.

I'm an avid listener of Not Another D&D Podcast, Critical Role and Dimension 20.

Video games

My favourites fall into two camps. Plot-driven narrative games — Mass Effect, Dragon Age, Jedi: Fallen Order and Survivor, and 007 First Light. And system-driven games where the fun is in understanding and optimising the machine — Dyson Sphere Program, Civilization and Transport Tycoon.

It's not a coincidence that those are the same two things I like about work: a story people can follow, and a system that actually holds together.
